# Vanilla+ front offices (plan)

Owner's call, 2026-11-11: scrap the title-odds trade engine as the core and
build on vanilla's. "Add direction for teams so they're motivated to move in
a certain direction like irl teams do. Market drives prices of trades but the
core is the vanilla trade engine dumbmatter built. Tweak it a bit to take
into account build and production but not a ton." Plus a news engine that
breaks stories about what teams are really doing. Free agency stays as it is
for now.

## Why

The realistic engine values every asset by how much it moves the team's own
title odds. For most teams in most seasons that is almost nothing, so:

- a middling team gave Murray, Gordon, a first and a prospect for Sabonis;
- Boston threw in all its 2031-33 firsts (worth 0.00 to it, 0.8-1.9% of a
  title to other teams);
- Charlotte would not take Jaylen Brown for a late second (he counted as a
  cost to it);
- Mobley for Tatum stalled under the margin of error.

Each patch (margin, frictions, market value) fixed one symptom and opened
another. Vanilla values a player by talent, age and contract, with one star
worth far more than several good players. It is crude but battle-tested, and
a vanilla team never hands a star away for nothing.

## What vanilla does today (the base)

- **Value** (team/ValueChangeCalculator.ts): each player's `value` (rating
  and potential blended by age) as a z-score, raised to the 7th power, so
  one star outweighs several good players. Picks: the players their likely
  slots produce. Contracts: a small bonus for a bargain, a penalty for an
  overpay. Injured players discounted. CPU teams overvalue their own players
  by 5%.
- **Direction** (team/updateStrategies.ts): two strategies, contending and
  rebuilding, from wins, the change in wins and roster age. Rebuilding
  teams value picks and players 23 and under up to ~10% more and players
  27+ up to 10% less; contending teams the reverse, more steeply (picks and
  teenagers 17-20% less). It barely changes behavior.
- **CPU-to-CPU trades** (trade/betweenAiTeams.ts): random. A random team
  offers a random asset to a random team, and makeItWork fills in the rest.
- **No idea of fit**: a team's needs, its positions and its build don't
  enter at all.

## The plan

### 1. Core: vanilla's machinery, players priced season by season

Vanilla's machinery behind one valuer: accepting a human's offer, "What
would make this work", the trading block, Trade Proposals, and every
CPU-to-CPU trade. A CPU team takes a deal when it comes out ahead by its
values, as in vanilla, at the league's trade difficulty. Kept: the free
agency overhaul (phases, cap rules, its screens), the vanilla trade screens,
multiplayer pieces. Retired from trades: the title-odds outlook, the margin
of error, trade frictions, the market-value floor.

**Worth, season by season** (worth.ts; owner's call "B", 2026-11-11).
Vanilla prices a player with one number, rating and potential blended by
age, raised to the 7th power. It blends before it raises, so a star who is
great now and fading later is priced as his average, and a teenager by a
potential rating that sits about 5 points above how careers actually turn
out (measured in random and real-player leagues alike). Vanilla+ prices each
season on its own instead:

- His next ten seasons are played out with the game's own development code
  (realisticFrontOffice/projection.ts, 40 careers, seeded per player and
  season, so every device agrees).
- Each season is worth vanilla's 7th power of the player he is then:
  `valueNoPot` moved by his projected rating change. It's the exact
  expectation over that season's spread of careers (Gauss-Hermite), so a
  chance at stardom counts for what it's worth without one lucky simulated
  career deciding it.
- The seasons are averaged with weights. The market's weights decline
  steadily (0.65 a season), set so that players in their prime cost what
  vanilla says (median ratio 1.00). Each lane uses its own window (section 2).
- Contracts, injuries, the CPU markup and makeItWork stay vanilla's. A
  contract is judged against the player's market worth.

Measured in the owner's league against vanilla's prices: aging stars cost
3-5× more (Murray ×5, Tatum ×3.7, Jokić ×3.4), high-potential youngsters
less (Robinson ÷4, Niang ÷6, Flagg ÷2.4, Wembanyama ÷1.8).

**Picks** come off the same scale: each draft class's prospects by their
seasons after the draft (getPickValues with vanilla+'s prospect value), and
drafts beyond the classes already generated by the farthest class, timed to
their own year. Vanilla's lottery odds and slot averaging are unchanged.

### 2. Direction: every team has a plan

Each team picks a lane, from the quality of its roster now and next season,
its core's age, its stars and its contracts:

| Lane         | Who                           | Wants                                        | Pays with                         |
| ------------ | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| All-in       | a real shot at the title now  | proven players, especially for its weak spot | picks, prospects, taking on money |
| Playoff team | good, not great               | upgrades that don't cost its future          | depth, seconds                    |
| Retooling    | aging or stuck in the middle  | younger starters, cap flexibility            | its older starters                |
| Rebuilding   | bad and young, or bad and old | picks, prospects, expiring deals             | its veterans                      |

- Lanes are sticky: a team changes only on clear evidence (a bad first half
  turns an all-in team into a seller at the deadline; a young team that
  breaks out moves up). Reviewed each offseason, in the preseason, and at
  30%, 45% and 60% of the season: a one-lane move waits for the 45% review,
  still before the deadline (it used to wait for the 60% one, after it, so a
  slumping team could never sell in time).
- In the season a team out of the race - further behind the last place that
  reaches the postseason in its conference (playoffs and play-in) than 12%
  of its games left, about 4 games at the deadline - is no playoff team: it
  retools (2026-11-12, after losing teams rated in the top half stayed
  "playoff" all season). A team at the play-in line in a weak conference is
  still in the race.
- A lane is a window on a player's seasons (worth.ts): an all-in team counts
  this season and next and a little of the third (1, 0.6, 0.25) and nothing
  after, since a 17-year-old's shot at stardom in year eight is no help to
  it; a playoff team five seasons tapering; a retooling team the market's
  decline a little slower (0.75); a rebuilding team the seasons it means to
  win in above this one (0.5 now, rising to 1 in years 3-5). Picks keep
  bounded lane factors (all-in 0.825 ... rebuilding 1.1, vanilla's).
- Buy at your own price, sell at the market's: a team never counts a player
  or pick it gives away below the market's worth, since it could always shop
  him instead (no more giveaways - a rebuilding team sells a veteran, it
  doesn't pay to dump him). To a human it never sells below what the
  keenest other team would pay, fit included: no buying a prospect cheap
  from a contender to sell him dear to a rebuilder.
- The lane also sets how busy a team is: all-in and rebuilding teams shop
  actively, playoff teams make smaller moves, retooling teams sell selectively.

### 3. Fit and production (small, capped tweaks)

- **Needs**: each team's weak spots from its rotation: no real center, no
  ball-handler, no shooting, thin defense. The position-aware lineup model
  the mode already has (strength.ts) measures how much a player would
  improve this team compared with a typical team.
- **Fit**: a player who fills a need is worth up to ~15% more to that team;
  one who duplicates what it has, up to ~15% less.
- **Aggression**: an all-in team with a glaring hole pays up to ~10% more
  again for a player who fills it, and goes looking for one (a good team
  without a center goes shopping for a center).
- **Production**: a player whose box-score impact over the last season and
  a half runs ahead of or behind his ratings is worth up to ~10% more or
  less. Ratings stay the base.

All tweaks together stay within about ±25% of vanilla's value, so the
vanilla core still decides.

### 4. A market that sets prices

CPU-to-CPU trades stop being random:

- **Buyers and sellers**: all-in and playoff teams with needs are buyers;
  rebuilding and retooling teams with veterans are sellers.
- **Targeted shopping**: a buyer asks the sellers who have a player for its
  need; a seller shops its veterans to the buyers who need them.
- **Best offer wins**: a team moving a star hears every interested team and
  takes the best offer by its own values, not the first one.
- **Timing**: busier before the deadline (sellers emerge from the standings)
  and in the offseason windows; quiet mid-season otherwise.
- Prices are vanilla+ values, settled the vanilla way (makeItWork).

### 5. The news engine

Stories come from what the backend really did or considered, never made up:

- **Direction changes**: "The Nuggets are open for business: Denver is
  listening on Jamal Murray and Aaron Gordon."
- **Needs**: "Contending Celtics canvassing the league for a starting center."
- **Talks**: when a seller gets several offers, "At least three teams are in
  on Murray."
- **Trades**, with why each side did it: "Why the Suns did it: Phoenix had no
  one to defend the four..."
- **Deadline**: buyers and sellers roundups, the market for the top names.
- **Later**: free-agent targets.

Rules:

- A rumor is only about a talk that really happened in the backend, but not
  every talk becomes a deal, as in real life.
- No exact valuations, no hidden ratings.
- CPU teams' plans only: a human team appears only as a party to a trade.
- A few stories a day league-wide at most, more around the deadline.

Written in the same AP style as the game recaps (headline plus a short
paragraph or two, with variety). In the UI: a "Front office" category in the
News Feed and a "RUMORS" slot in the bottom ticker. Nothing else added.

## How it's checked

- **Test leagues** (arena): trades a season (NBA ~50-60 including small
  ones), stars moved, giveaways (a 62+ player for no 55+ player and no
  first), how spread out records get (the current engine pulls the league
  apart), title concentration, how long rebuilds take, and how often a
  contender with a hole fills it.
- **Exploit hunting**: the shark bot against vanilla+, and the owner's known
  fleeces (aging star for picks, trading up cheaply, trading down) as tests.
- **The owner's cases as regression tests**: Denver-Murray, Charlotte-Brown,
  Boston's picks, Mobley-Tatum.
- **Speed**: no title-odds outlook in trades, so it should be faster.

## What the checks found (2026-11-11)

- **The owner's cases**, replayed in the owner's league (preseason 2027,
  the owner's ratings and contracts):
  - Denver turns down Murray + Gordon + two seconds for Sabonis.
  - Charlotte takes Brown for its first second.
  - Boston's 2031-33 firsts are each worth something to it (never zero).
  - Boston takes Tatum for Mobley, by a mile: in vanilla's values, Mobley
    (70/73, 26) is worth several Tatums (67/69, 29).
- **"What would make this work"** used to have stock's balancing hand over a
  whole bench to even out a star deal. Boston offered Tatum, 10 players and
  every pick for Mobley. Now no deal leaves a roster past the limit, or past
  where it already is in the offseason.
- **Lanes in the owner's league**: 6 all-in, 11 playoff, 4 retool, 9
  rebuild. A team's age is now read from its best eight players now, not by

- **Exploits**: all of trade/exploits.test.ts passes with vanilla+ on. The
  owner's fleeces are now tests (vanillaPlus/exploits.test.ts):
  - an all-in team's aging star isn't sold for three firsts;
  - no pick is given away for nothing;
  - no one-for-one swap is taken both ways.
- **Prospects** (before worth season by season): vanilla priced raw
  teenagers by potential, so in the owner's league a 17-year-old 36/76

  player in the rest of Murray's deal, 22% in five years, 36% in eight. The
  owner chose to price players by their seasons (section 1). Now Denver's
  worth of Robinson is about nothing (0.5 against Murray's 40, owner's

- **The middleman** (vanillaPlus/exploits.test.ts): without the rule that a
  human pays at least the keenest other team's price, a human could buy a

  as much; with it, at most 0.8.
- **Test leagues** (2 seeds, 3 seasons; before worth season by season):
  - Trades: 35-37 in season 1, then 10-21 a season.
  - The CPU markup barely matters: 2% vs 5% gives about the same volume,
    and 0%/1%/2% in settled leagues all give 7-11 trades in 40 days. What
    limits deals is vanilla's own search: the team that shops often doesn't
    want the best price it's quoted.
  - Haggling (refuse a piece, ask again) changed nothing and was dropped.
  - Giveaways: 1-2 per 50 trades, all rebuild-lane deals (veterans for young
    prospects).
  - Seasons run in ~7.5 minutes against ~14 for the current engine.
  - Records spread out over the seasons: win SD 13-14, then 16-17, then
    17-18. The current engine's test leagues on the same seeds do the same
    (12.9-13.8, then 13.8-17.0, then 16.2-18.1), so it's free agency, not
    trades. The current engine made 10-19 trades a season against
    vanilla+'s 16-36, and took 10-22 minutes a season.
  - News: about 37 stories a season.

- **Test leagues, worth season by season** (same seeds, 3 seasons):
  - B (prices by season): 25-33 trades in season 1, then 10-14; 6.2-7.1

- **With vanilla+ on**, every worker test passes but one that fails on
  master too (sync/notifications); the title-odds engine's own trade tests

- **Switched on** 2026-11-12 (cloud change 282).

## Next: a market that never stops (plan, 2026-11-12)

The owner: "Teams are just constantly shopping around, and the deadline is
just when push comes to shove... their patience grows smaller as the
deadline approaches. And it all based on the market: a CPU team should never
trade me a package if it could go to some other team and get a higher
package."

Measured now (test leagues, seasons 2-3): when teams trade, they mostly
trade the right way (60% of deals between a clearly better and a clearly
worse team send the better player now to the better team, 10% the other
way), but too few do it: about 2 of the 8 best teams buy a 60+ player a
season and 1 of the 8 worst sells a starter, against roughly 4-6 and 5-8
in an NBA season. The market picks a couple of teams a day at random to
shop, more in the last week; a contender with a hole may never get a turn.

### 1. What a player is worth knows about his contract and the calendar

Two things worth.ts doesn't count yet, and both are what makes the
deadline matter:

- **Seasons past his contract** count only by the chance he re-signs (the
  free agency model's stay chance, cached and refreshed every couple of
  weeks of the season). A rental - a veteran on an expiring deal - is worth
  this season to a contender and next to nothing to a rebuilding team, so
  the rebuilding team wants him gone before the deadline, as real ones do.
- **This season is the rest of the regular season plus the playoffs, and the
  playoffs count most** (owner: "playoffs are by far most important as long
  as the team is in a place to make it / have a decent seed"). This season's
  worth to a team is the regular season still to play (the seed race) plus
  the playoffs times the team's chance of being there - more for a better
  seed. A team in position counts the playoffs fully; a team out of it
  counts only the games left, and those against it once losing helps its own
  pick (vanilla's pick projection already moves with a roster's strength, so
  selling a starter raises the value of the seller's own pick). For the
  market price, the league-average chance, so prime players still cost what
  vanilla says.

**Injuries** (owner: "if a contender suffers an awful injury, other
contenders should be far more willing to go all in if it puts them over the
top"): a team is rated now from the players back for at least half the
games left, and in the playoffs from those back by then. Its playoffs are
getting there plus its title shot against the other likely playoff teams by
playoff strength, so a rival's star out for the year raises everyone else's
stake in this season, most for the teams just behind it. An injured player's
own worth counts only the games he'll be back for, and what's left of the
injury next season, in place of stock's flat discount (so a star out for the
season keeps his worth past it: to a contender he's worth little, to a
rebuilder still a lot - stock's "a season-ending injury costs a good player
a great deal" holds for contenders only).

### 2. Patience: wait only when waiting is worth more than acting

A team doesn't trade just because a deal clears even. It weighs acting now
against waiting:

- **Waiting can find a better deal**: early in the season there's time to
  shop, so a team asks for a margin (a share of what it gives up, starting
  around 20%, calibrated to the NBA's timing), falling through the season to
  about 1% on deadline day - push comes to shove. Never zero, so no team
  trades back and forth.
- **Waiting also costs**: part of a deal's value melts as games are played -
  a seed race, a tank, a rental's remaining games (section 1). That part is
  set against the margin, so a deal that's worth most done now gets done
  now: a bad team selling its starter early to tank for a better pick, a good
  team adding a player early to push for a high seed (owner: "not
  necessarily never worth it early; sometimes it's necessary to do early").
- After the deadline, no trades until the offseason; then a gentler curve
  through the draft and free agency.

Why a share of what's given: a fixed amount would block every small deal and
barely touch big ones.

**Built (setting `patience`, off until measured):** calendar.ts `patience`
is 20% before the season, falling with the square of the distance left to
the deadline (`tradeDeadline`, the share of the season it falls at) to 1% on
deadline day; 6% through the draft and free agency. ValueChangeCalculator
takes it as `margin`: a deal must come out ahead by that share of what the
team gives (`valuesRemove`). Searches price their pieces without it
(`raw`), so what a team offers isn't shrunk twice. Checked
(vanillaPlus.test.ts): of the one-for-ones a CPU team would take on worth
alone, patience turns many down at the start of the season and few at the
deadline, and never makes a deal look better.

### 3. Everyone shops, all the time

The point is a market that's really happening (owner: "most important is
that it actually gets done so the market is active"), at a sim speed that
stays reasonable. Every team looks at the market regularly instead of a
couple a day at random: buyers for the players that would help them most, sellers for the
best home for players they'd sell. Early in the season a team looks every
week or so, rising to every day in the last weeks. Most looks find nothing
good enough for the patience of the day, so deals pile up toward the
deadline by themselves - no deadline rule needed. Speed is budgeted: a look
reuses the day's prices, about a tenth of a second.

**Built (setting `patience`):** in the regular season every CPU team shops
on a day with a chance of 10% early, rising with the square of the way to
the deadline to 50% on deadline day, times its lane's activity against the
league's (market.ts `shoppersToday`); a team that traded waits 5 days
instead of 10. Free agency keeps its 3 shoppers a day. The arena reports
looks, trades and the market's time per season (`vplusMarket`).

**Offseason windows (2026-11-12):** vanilla+ traded only in the season and
in free agency - the old engine's windows run only with vanilla+ off - so
CPU teams made 0-3 trades an offseason, all in free agency (arena P, seeds
391-394), against the NBA's 28-39. Now every CPU team gets a look, in a
random order, as the draft, the time after it, the re-signing period and
the preseason open (market.ts `tradeWindow`, from phase/finalize.ts).

**Clearing cap room on vanilla+'s values (2026-11-12):** free agency's room
trades (realisticFrontOffice/clearRoom.ts) were found and priced by the old
engine: a 51-31 team gave two firsts and two young players to clear $5.5M,
and teams taking salary got picks they'd have done the deal without. With
vanilla+ on (vanillaPlus/clearRoom.ts) the team taking the players must
want them as in any trade, with the fewest of the plan's picks that does
it, and the team clearing room must value the free agent, at his ask,
above everything it gives; the trade is checked again the same way when he
signs.

### 4. The market sets the price: every seller shops an offer around

As any real market works (owner: "I'm not just going to take this smaller
amount if that team would pay more").

When a team is offered a deal for its player - by a CPU team or a human -
it asks the other teams that would want him what they'd give (their best
offers, the value-for-money search that's live now) and takes the best.
So:

- **Built (with the calendar): a human never gets a player for less than
  any other team's best real offer** (market.ts `bestOfferElsewhere`).
  Every other CPU team makes its best offer: the package of up to four of
  its players and picks worth most to his team that still leaves it ahead
  (`packagesFor`/`firstThatWorks`: searched on each piece's worth, which
  adds up very nearly, then checked against the real values, dropping the
  smallest pieces when the sums miss). His team won't take less from a
  human than the best of them, in its own terms - as it values anything it
  gets - after contracts. No floor where nobody would bid (a bad contract
  stays a cost).
  - The first version asked only the four keenest teams, and filled their
    offers by value for money: the slots went on scraps, so a rebuilder
    worth 23 for a prospect offered 5, and a rebuilder with what a contender
    wanted most (its veterans, 315 for a star the contender priced at 140)
    wasn't asked. Every team, best package: a human pays at least every
    team's real offer, exactly (exploits.test.ts).
  - The keenest team's own worth for a player (the floor without the
    calendar) isn't used with it: a contender's worth for a player this
    season is no price a contender could pay (it put Tatum at 171 against
    Mobley's 157 for Boston, in the owner's league).
  - Valuing other teams' packages at what the seller could sell them on for
    was tried: it would value a rebuilder's picks above a human's, so a
    human would pay more for being human. Left out.
  - What's left of the middleman: lanes value things differently, so a
    human reselling a player to the team that wants him most can make a
    little - at most about 14% on a real player (exploits.test.ts holds it
    under 20%), more only on bench players a team values at almost nothing.
  - Speed: pieces are priced once per pricing and shared; a team that
    couldn't beat the best offer so far isn't asked. Pricing a team's first
    player for a human about 0.2 s, each after about 5 ms; the trading block
    4-5 s (2 s without the calendar).
  - The same best-package search is a seller's buyers' bids in the CPU
    market (calendar on).
- **A human never gets a package another team would beat.** The CPU team
  compares the human's offer with the best any other CPU team would give for
  the same players, in its own values, and takes the human's only if it's at
  least as good and clears the day's patience. This replaces the current
  rule (each player priced at the keenest other team's worth): it judges the
  whole package, picks included, and what other teams could actually pay.
- **Between CPU teams too:** a buyer's offer gets shopped around to the other
  interested teams before the seller says yes, and the news reports it ("at
  least three teams made offers").
  **Built (setting `auctions`, off; market.ts `auction`):** when a buyer's
  shop finds a deal, the seller hears the four teams keenest on the player
  (their best real offers). If one beats the buyer's offer, the team that
  would pay most - the buyer at its most, or the rival - gets him, paying
  just enough to beat the other (second price). Checked: a few market days
  with everything on trade, outbid buyers and keep rosters legal.
- Cost: a human's offer to a team prices the alternatives once (a second or
  two); "What would make this work" and the trading block reuse it.

### 5. Then measure

- Trades a season (NBA about 50-60 including the offseason), and the share
  of in-season deals in the final week or two.
- Of the 8 best teams, how many add a player; of the 8 worst, how many sell;
  rentals moved before the deadline.
- The owner's four cases, both exploit suites (a human can't beat the best
  CPU offer, can't flip a player for profit, no trade-backs), and speed.
- Then firsts: whether contenders now pay with them, or their own firsts
  need to count for less inside their window.

Order: 1, then 2 and 3 together (patience with nobody shopping does
nothing), then 4, measuring each in test leagues before the next.
